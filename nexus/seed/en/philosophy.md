# Introduction to Philosophy
categoria: Philosophy

An overview of the main philosophical schools and ideas, with practical applications.

## What philosophy is
Philosophy ("love of wisdom" in Greek) is the rational inquiry into fundamental questions: what exists (metaphysics), what we can know (epistemology), how we should act (ethics), what is beautiful (aesthetics), how to reason correctly (logic) and how to organize life together (political philosophy).

## Socrates (469–399 BC)
He wrote nothing; we know him through Plato and Xenophon. The Socratic method: asking successive questions to expose contradictions and reach better definitions. Associated sayings: "I know that I know nothing" and "The unexamined life is not worth living". He held that no one does wrong knowingly — wrongdoing comes from ignorance. He was sentenced to death for "corrupting the youth".
Application: question your certainties by asking "why?" several times before deciding.

## Plato (427–347 BC)
Student of Socrates, founder of the Academy. Theory of Forms: the physical world is an imperfect copy of a realm of perfect, eternal ideas. The Allegory of the Cave: prisoners see only shadows and take them for reality; the philosopher is the one who leaves the cave and sees the light. In "The Republic" he proposes a just city ruled by philosopher-kings and divides the soul into reason, spirit and appetite.

## Aristotle (384–322 BC)
Student of Plato and tutor of Alexander the Great. He valued observation of the concrete world. He created formal logic (the syllogism: "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal"). Nicomachean Ethics: the goal of life is eudaimonia (flourishing), achieved through virtue — the mean between two extremes (courage lies between cowardice and recklessness). Virtues are built by habit: we become what we repeatedly do.

## Stoicism
Founded by Zeno of Citium (c. 300 BC); key figures: Epictetus (a former slave), Seneca (adviser to Nero) and Marcus Aurelius (Roman emperor, author of "Meditations").
Core ideas:
- Dichotomy of control (Epictetus): some things are up to us (judgments, intentions, actions); others are not (body, reputation, outcomes, other people). Focus your energy on what is up to you.
- It is not things that disturb us, but our judgments about them.
- Virtue (wisdom, courage, justice and temperance) is the only true good.
- Premeditatio malorum: imagine difficulties in advance so you can face them calmly.
- Memento mori: remember death to value your time.
- Amor fati: accept, and even love, whatever happens.
Daily practice: in the morning, prepare for the day's challenges; at night, review it (what did I do well, what badly, what did I leave undone — Seneca's exercise).

## Epicureanism
Epicurus (341–270 BC): pleasure is the highest good, understood as absence of bodily pain (aponia) and of mental disturbance (ataraxia). He praised simple pleasures, friendship and reflection, and distinguished natural and necessary desires (food, shelter, friends) from empty ones (fame, luxury). On death: "Where death is, I am not; where I am, death is not."

## Eastern philosophy (brief)
Confucius: ethics of relationships, respect, self-cultivation and the golden rule (do not do to others what you would not want done to you). Taoism (Laozi): harmony with the Tao, simplicity, wu wei (effortless action). Buddhism: the Four Noble Truths (suffering exists, it arises from attachment, it can cease, and there is a path — the Noble Eightfold Path); the impermanence of all things.

## Descartes and rationalism
René Descartes (1596–1650) used methodical doubt: doubt everything that can be doubted. One certainty remained: "I think, therefore I am" (cogito, ergo sum). Dualism: mind and body are distinct substances. Rationalists (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz) trust reason as the main source of knowledge.

## Empiricism
Locke, Berkeley and Hume: all knowledge comes from experience; the mind starts as a "blank slate". David Hume questioned causation (we observe sequences of events, not a necessary connection) and raised the problem of induction: nothing logically guarantees the future will resemble the past.

## Kant and the ethics of duty
Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) bridged rationalism and empiricism: knowledge depends on experience but is organized by structures of the mind (space, time, causality). Deontological ethics: act from duty, following the Categorical Imperative:
1. Act only according to a rule you could will to become a universal law.
2. Treat humanity, in yourself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means.

## Utilitarianism
Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill: the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number (consequentialism). Mill distinguished higher (intellectual) from lower pleasures. Common criticism: it may justify sacrificing individuals for the majority (e.g. the trolley problem).

## Nietzsche
Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) proclaimed the "death of God": the collapse of traditional values and the risk of nihilism. He proposed the revaluation of all values, the will to power, the Übermensch (who creates their own values) and eternal recurrence: live as if you had to relive this life infinitely.

## Existentialism
Kierkegaard (anxiety, leap of faith), Sartre ("existence precedes essence": we are not born with a fixed purpose; we create ourselves through our choices — "condemned to be free"), Simone de Beauvoir ("One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman") and Albert Camus (the absurd: we seek meaning in a silent universe; the answer is lucid revolt — "one must imagine Sisyphus happy").

## Logic and common fallacies
An argument is valid when the conclusion follows from the premises; it is sound when, in addition, the premises are true.
Frequent fallacies:
- Ad hominem: attacking the person instead of the argument.
- Straw man: distorting someone's argument to refute it easily.
- False dilemma: presenting only two options when others exist.
- Appeal to authority: "it's true because X said so" (without evidence).
- Bandwagon: "everyone does it, so it's right".
- Slippery slope: assuming one step will inevitably lead to a chain of disasters.
- Post hoc: "it happened after, so it was caused by it".
- Hasty generalization: drawing a general conclusion from a few cases.
Occam's razor: among equally good explanations, prefer the simplest.
